Couples spend real time choosing a processional song, then realize there's music playing for the fifteen minutes before it — and no plan for what that should be. The prelude is the first music your guests actually hear, quietly shaping the mood before a word is spoken, so it's worth a few minutes of thought rather than being left to whatever a playlist happens to queue up.

## What Is Wedding Prelude Music?

The prelude is the music played as guests arrive and find their seats, running until the processional begins. It sits in the background rather than commanding attention — its job is to settle the room and signal that the ceremony is about to start, not to announce a specific moment the way the processional or recessional does. Live piano or strings work especially well here, since the sound can rise and fall naturally as guests trickle in, something a fixed playlist can't do.

## How Long Does a Wedding Prelude Last?

Most preludes run 10 to 15 minutes, timed to how long guests take to arrive and be seated rather than a fixed length. A venue with a longer walk from parking or a wider arrival window often needs closer to 15 to 20 minutes of music; a smaller, more contained ceremony space can need less. We confirm the actual timing once we know your venue and expected guest flow, so the music doesn't run out before your seats are full or end awkwardly early.

## What Should You Play During the Prelude?

A short set of two or three quieter pieces covers most preludes comfortably. Because the prelude sits lower in energy than the rest of the ceremony, it's a natural home for pieces that are pretty but not attention-grabbing:

| Style                         | Example                                       | Why it fits the prelude                             |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Classical standard            | Debussy — Clair de Lune                       | Quiet, familiar, doesn't compete with conversation  |
| Baroque                       | Bach — Jesu, Joy of Man's Desiring            | Steady and gentle, works well as background         |
| Soft contemporary arrangement | A piano arrangement of a favourite film theme | Warmer and more personal without raising the energy |

You're not limited to this list — see [Repertoire](/repertoire/) for the fuller range of pieces we arrange for piano and strings, including options for the prelude specifically.

## Does the Prelude Need to Match the Rest of the Ceremony?

No. The prelude, processional, and recessional can be three completely different pieces, and most ceremonies work that way without any issue. Some couples do like the continuity of returning to a prelude piece later — often during the signing or vow exchange — which gives the ceremony a quiet musical thread without requiring it. Neither approach is more "correct"; it comes down to whether you want that thread or not.

## Solo Piano or Strings for the Prelude?

Either works, and the choice usually follows whatever formation you've already chosen for the rest of the ceremony rather than being decided separately. A solo pianist covers the prelude comfortably on its own — quiet, intimate, and enough to fill a garden or smaller venue while guests arrive. If you've booked a Duo, Trio, or String Quartet, the same musicians typically ease into the prelude together, often starting with just one or two instruments and layering in as more guests arrive.
