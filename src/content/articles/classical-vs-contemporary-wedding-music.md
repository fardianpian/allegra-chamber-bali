Choosing between classical and contemporary music for your ceremony can feel like picking a side before you've even started planning. In practice, the two work together more often than not — here's how each style sounds on chamber instruments, when each one fits, and how to describe what you're picturing so we can build the right program with you.

## What "Classical" and "Contemporary" Actually Mean for a Chamber Ensemble

"Classical" and "contemporary" describe two different sources for the same repertoire, not two different sounds. Classical pieces were originally written for piano, strings, or orchestra — pieces like Canon in D or Air on the G String need no adaptation to work on a chamber ensemble, because chamber instruments are what they were composed for. Contemporary pieces were written for other instruments entirely — a pop vocal, a film score, a guitar — and are arranged for piano and strings before a live ensemble can play them. Both end up sounding like they belong in the same ceremony once they're arranged, which is part of why most couples end up mixing the two rather than picking one.

## When Classical Pieces Fit Best

Classical pieces tend to fit best for the prelude and processional — moments where a steady, familiar structure helps set the tone before the ceremony itself begins. Pieces such as Canon in D, Clair de Lune, or Ave Maria carry an established, easily recognisable character that reads as formal without needing lyrics to do the work. They also suit ceremonies with a more traditional structure, where a recognisable classical piece signals the moment clearly to guests. None of this rules out a classical prelude followed by a completely different tone later in the day — it's a fit for a moment, not a fixed style for the whole ceremony.

## When Contemporary Arrangements Fit Best

Contemporary arrangements tend to fit best where you want the music to say something specific about you as a couple — the processional, the recessional, or a moment built around a song that means something to your relationship. A first-dance song, a film theme from something you watched together, or a pop track arranged for strings and piano carries a different kind of meaning than a classical standard: guests who know the original recognise it immediately, and the arrangement makes it sound like it was always meant for a wedding ceremony. Recessionals in particular lean contemporary more often, since the mood after the vows tends to be brighter and more celebratory than the prelude.

## Why Most Ceremonies Mix Both Across Prelude, Processional, and Recessional

Most ceremonies use both styles rather than committing to one. A common structure is a classical prelude while guests are seated, a contemporary arrangement — often the couple's own song — for the processional, and something brighter for the recessional, whether that's classical or contemporary. There's no rule requiring consistency across the ceremony's different moments; what matters is that each piece fits the tone of that specific moment.
