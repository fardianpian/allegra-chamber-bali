A chapel changes two things couples don't always expect: how sound behaves inside an enclosed
space, and where musicians physically fit near a narrow aisle. This guide covers chapel acoustics,
standing room, church music guidelines, and formation choice, so you can walk into your venue
meeting already knowing what to ask.

## How Chapel Acoustics Shape Live Piano and Strings

A chapel's walls and ceiling do far more acoustic work than an open lawn or beachfront ever can —
sound reflects and fills the room instead of spreading outward and thinning out. That usually
means live piano and strings read clearly and warmly in a small chapel with little or no help from
amplification, even for a fuller formation. A larger stone or high-ceilinged chapel holds more
natural reverb than a small timber one, which can make a slower, more spacious piece sit better
than a fast one — something we factor into the program once we know your venue.

## Where the Musicians Stand in a Small Chapel

Most small chapels place musicians to one side near the altar or in a side aisle rather than in
the main aisle itself, so the most compact formations fit this footprint most easily. Every
musician still needs flat, stable floor space for themselves, their instrument, and a stand, and
piano — included as standard in Duo, Trio, and String Quartet — needs its own level surface near a
power outlet if it isn't an in-house instrument. Confirming the exact spot with your venue or
planner during a walkthrough avoids surprises on the day; our [For Planners &
Venues](/for-planners/) page covers what we need from your planning team to make that confirmation
straightforward.

## Church and Catholic Ceremonies: Music Rules to Ask About Early

Church and Catholic ceremonies in Bali follow the rules of that specific church, not one fixed
policy shared across every chapel — so this is a conversation to start early, not an assumption to
make. Some churches set guidelines around what can be played during particular moments of the
service; others leave the music program largely up to the couple. Share your church or officiant's
guidelines with us as soon as you have them, and we'll build the ceremony program to fit within
them rather than working around a conflict discovered late.

## Church Wedding Entrance and Exit Songs

Classical pieces written for piano and strings are the most common choice for both ends of a
church ceremony, because they need no adaptation to suit the instruments. For the entrance, Bach's
Air on the G String, Pachelbel's Canon in D, and Wagner's Bridal Chorus are all steady, familiar
choices that suit an unhurried walk down the aisle. For the exit, Mendelssohn's Wedding March is
the traditional choice, bright and brief. Our [repertoire page](/repertoire/) lists the full
collection, and we confirm the final selection against your specific church's music guidelines
before the day, since what's allowed can vary from one church to the next.

## Choosing a Formation for Chapel Size

The right formation depends on your chapel's size and how close the first rows sit to the altar,
not on the fact that it's an indoor religious venue:

| Formation                              | Best fit for a chapel ceremony                                                                     |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Solo (violin, cello, or piano)         | A very small chapel, or a ceremony where one instrument carrying the whole program fits best       |
| Duo (violin, cello & piano)            | A small-to-mid chapel where piano holds the harmony while violin and cello carry the melody        |
| Trio / String Quartet (piano included) | A larger chapel with more aisle length, more seating, or a ceremony moving into a longer reception |

Piano is a flagship part of every one of these formations, not an add-on reserved for the largest
option — Duo, Trio, and String Quartet all include it as standard. See [Packages](/packages/) for
the complete formation lineup, including Large Ensemble for a reception that follows the ceremony.

## Moving From Chapel Ceremony to Reception

Many chapel weddings move straight from the ceremony into cocktail hour or a reception on the same
property, and the musicians can carry on through that transition without a separate booking. A
Solo or Duo used for the chapel ceremony itself can continue as background music for cocktail hour,
or step up to a larger formation if the reception calls for a fuller sound. Tell us your full-day
timeline when you inquire, including how the chapel ceremony connects to whatever comes next, and
we'll confirm a formation and setup that works across both.
