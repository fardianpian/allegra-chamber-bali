The ceremony ends and suddenly you need music for guests holding champagne and catching up before dinner — a question many couples haven't thought through when they book. Cocktail hour calls for a different feel than the aisle: here's what actually changes, so you can plan it alongside your ceremony rather than as an afterthought.

## How Cocktail Hour Music Differs From Ceremony Music

Ceremony music is built around distinct, timed moments — the processional, the vow exchange, the recessional — where a piece starts and ends with something specific happening. Cocktail hour works differently: it's a continuous backdrop for guests mingling, holding drinks, and moving between spaces, so the music runs as one longer, uninterrupted set rather than a sequence of cued pieces. The musicians are typically the same ones booked for your ceremony, but the role the music plays — and the pacing — shifts from marking moments to setting a mood.

## Does the Same Formation Continue, or Does the Sound Change?

In most cases, yes — the same musicians who play your ceremony can continue straight into a cocktail hour set without changing instrumentation. Our [wedding pianist guide](/journal/wedding-pianist-bali/) confirms this for Solo piano, and it holds for our other formations too: Duo, Trio, and String Quartet are all built to cover ceremony through cocktail hour as standard. See our [packages page](/packages/) for which formations list cocktail hour among their typical uses. What changes isn't the ensemble, but the repertoire and pacing — covered next.

## Repertoire That Works for a Relaxed, Conversational Setting

Cocktail hour repertoire favors pieces that support conversation rather than draw attention to themselves — lighter and more conversational than the processional or recessional pieces chosen for the ceremony itself. Our repertoire spans a few different moods for this stretch of the day: classical pieces like Saint-Saëns' Le Cygne (The Swan) and Elgar's Salut d'Amour sit alongside film themes such as Michael Giacchino's Married Life from Up and Joe Hisaishi's Summer, and jazz-era standards like Fly Me to the Moon and La Vie en Rose. None of it is written to compete for attention — the goal is a comfortable backdrop, not a performance guests are asked to watch. Piano tends to carry this stretch of the day especially well: it holds the harmony and rhythm on its own the way it does across our formations, so a cocktail hour set can lean on solo piano or piano alongside strings without ever sounding thin. Our [repertoire page](/repertoire/) shows the fuller list by moment, including what's tagged for cocktail hour specifically.

## Can You Book Ceremony and Cocktail Hour Together?

Yes — pairing ceremony and cocktail hour into one continuous booking is one of the most common requests we get, since your musicians are already set up and simply continue playing rather than packing up and returning later. It's treated as one booking with an extended scope, not two separate bookings, which is usually simpler for your venue and timeline as well. If you're also weighing whether to add a band or DJ once dancing starts later in the evening, our [wedding band vs. DJ vs. chamber ensemble guide](/journal/wedding-band-vs-dj/) breaks down how a chamber ensemble typically hands off once the reception moves into dancing.

## What to Tell Us About Your Cocktail Hour Setup

A few details help us plan cocktail hour music alongside your ceremony: whether it's in the same location as your ceremony or a separate space, roughly how long you want it covered, and any songs or moods you'd like included beyond what we'd typically choose. If cocktail hour moves to a different area of the venue — a garden or terrace away from the ceremony space — let us know so we can plan setup and access for that second location. Share these details when you [reach out](/contact/) or send them with your booking inquiry, and we'll confirm scope and set structure together.
