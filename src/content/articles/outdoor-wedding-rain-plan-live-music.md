An outdoor Bali ceremony can look perfect in the forecast until a shower rolls in before the processional — and piano and strings simply can't play through it. This guide covers the rain plan to agree before the date, so a shower changes where the music happens, not whether it happens.

## Why Live Instruments Need Their Own Rain Plan

Piano and string instruments can't be played in the rain, so an outdoor ceremony needs a weather plan that's separate from any general wet-weather backup for guests and catering. The safety of the musicians and their instruments comes first, which is why the plan isn't "wait and see" — it's a specific, agreed move made before conditions turn. [Keeping Live Wedding Instruments Safe From Bali's Heat and Rain](/journal/wedding-instruments-heat-rain-bali/) goes into why: water on varnish and bare wood is exactly the moisture these instruments are built to avoid, and even a light drizzle counts.

## Choosing a Covered Spot With the Venue in Advance

The covered spot is agreed with your planner or venue before the date, not chosen after clouds roll in. It needs to be genuinely covered and dry, not just shaded — a shade sail or open-sided tent that blocks sun doesn't necessarily block wind-blown rain. Beyond that, it should sit close enough to the original ceremony spot that guests can follow the move easily, hold the formation comfortably, and offer a dry, covered power connection if the booking includes piano. Most venues already have a nearby covered deck, tent, or indoor room that works for exactly this purpose.

## Who Makes the Weather Call, and When

Moving under cover is a shared call between the musicians and your planner or venue on-site, using the spot you agreed on in advance — not a decision either side makes alone once the ceremony is already underway. Rain is treated as a firm stop rather than a judgment call, so the move happens as soon as rain looks likely, not after instruments have already been exposed to it. Confirming the backup spot ahead of time is what makes that call quick instead of improvised.

## Wind: The Other Weather Risk

Wind is worth planning for separately from rain. It changes how far live sound carries to seated guests more than it puts the instruments themselves at risk, so a windier or more exposed setting — a clifftop or open beach, for instance — is where we start the amplification conversation with you. Heat and humidity carry a different kind of risk, to the wood and glue of the instruments themselves, and that's covered in full in [Keeping Live Wedding Instruments Safe From Bali's Heat and Rain](/journal/wedding-instruments-heat-rain-bali/) — worth reading alongside this rain plan rather than repeating here.

## What to Put in the Rain Plan Section of Your Run Sheet

A rain plan only works on the day if it's written down ahead of it, not held as a verbal understanding. The rain plan section of your run sheet should cover:

- The exact covered backup location, confirmed with the venue by name or description
- Who agreed to it — planner, venue, and musicians, all aware of the same spot
- How guests move or are guided to the backup spot, if the ceremony relocates mid-program
- A dry, covered power connection at the backup spot, if your formation includes piano
- A note that the call to move is made jointly on-site, not waited out
